## 10.17

:::{.question}

> A new curing process developed for a certain type of cement results
> in a mean compressive strength of 5000 kilograms per square
> centimeter with a standard deviation of 120 kilograms. To test the
> hypothesis that `μ = 5000` against the alternative that `μ < 5000`,
> a random sample of 50 pieces of cement is tested. The critical
> region is defined to be $\bar X < 4970$. \
> \
> (a) Find the probability of committing a type I error when `H0` is
> true. \
> (b) Evaluate `β` for the alternatives `μ = 4970` and `μ = 4960`.

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

\text{H0} &\longleftarrow \mu = 5000 \\
\text{H1} &\longleftarrow \mu < 5000 \\
\sigma &= 120 \text{kg} \\
n &= 50 > 30 \implies \bar X \sim N(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{sample size is large enough to be approx. normal}} \\
\\
\alpha &= \mathbb{P}(\bar X < 4970 | \mu = 5000) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{(a) type I err.}} \\
       &= \Phi(Z < z) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{cdf of standard normal distribution}} \\
z &= \frac{\bar X - \mu}{sigma / \sqrt{n}} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{standardize $\bar X$}} \\
  &= \frac{4970 - 5000}{120 / \sqrt{50}} \\
z &\approx -1.77 \\
\alpha &= \Phi(-1.77) \approx 0.0384 \space_\blacksquare \\
\\
\beta &= \mathbb{P}(\bar X \geq 4970 | \mu = \text{alt.}) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{(b) type II err. for alts 4970 \& 4960}} \\
\text{when}\space \mu &= 4970 \\
              z &= 0 \implies \beta = \mathbb{P}(Z \geq 0) \\
\text{therefore}\space \beta &= 0.5 \space_\blacksquare \\
\text{when}\space \mu &= 4960 \\
              z &= \frac{4970 - 4960}{120 / \sqrt{50}} \\
              z &\approx 0.59 \implies \beta = \mathbb{P}(Z \geq 0.59) \\
\text{therefore}\space \beta &= 1 - \Phi(0.59) \approx 1 - 0.7224 \\
                       \beta &\approx 0.2776 \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$

## 10.25

:::{.question}

> It is claimed that automobiles are driven on average more than
> 20,000 kilometers per year. To test this claim, 100 randomly
> selected automobile owners are asked to keep a record of the
> kilometers they travel. Would you agree with this claim if the
> random sample showed an average of 23,500 kilometers and a standard
> deviation of 3900 kilometers? Use a P-value in your conclusion.

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

\text{H0:} \mu &= 20000 \\
\text{H1:} \mu &> 20000 \\
\\
\mu_0 &= 20000 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{null mean}} \\
\bar x &= 23500 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{sample mean}} \\
n &= 100 \\
s &= 3900 \\
\\
z &= \frac{23500 - 20000}{3900/\sqrt{100}} = \frac{3500}{390} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{test statistic for z-test}} \\
z &\approx 8.97 \\
\\
P &= P(Z > 8.97) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{one-tailed test of normal population}} \\
P &\approx 0 \implies \text{$\bar x$ is well outside critical region} \\
  &\text{reject $H0$} \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$

## 10.27

:::{.question}

> A study at the University of Colorado at Boulder shows that running
> increases the percent resting metabolic rate (RMR) in older women.
> The average RMR of 30 elderly women runners was 34.0% higher than
> the average RMR of 30 sedentary elderly women, and the standard
> deviations were reported to be 10.5 and 10.2%, respectively. Was
> there a significant increase in RMR of the women runners over the
> sedentary women? Assume the populations to be approximately
> normally distributed with equal variances. Use a P-value in your
> conclusions.

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

\text{$\bar{X_1}$:}&\space\text{elderly women runners} \\
\text{$\bar{X_2}$:}&\space\text{sedentary elderly women} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{both assumed normally distributed}} \\
n_1 &= n_2 = 30 \\
s_1 &= 10.5\% \\
s_2 &= 10.2\% &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{variances are similar}} \\
\bar{x_1} &- \bar{x_2} = 34.0\% \\
d_0 &= 0.0\% \\
\\
\text{H0:} &\mu_1 = \mu2 \\
\text{H1:} &\mu_1 - \mu2 > 0 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{one-tailed hypothesis}} \\
\\
s_p^2 &= \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{pooled variance}} \\
      &= \frac{29(10.5^2)+29(10.2^2)}{58} \\
s_p^2 &\approx 107.1 \\
s_p &= \sqrt{s_p^2} \approx 10.35 \\
\\
t &= \frac{(\bar{x_1} - \bar{x_2}) - d_0}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{normal dist., sim. var. $\implies$ pooled t-test}} \\
  &\approx \frac{34.0 - 0}{10.35\sqrt{1/15}} \\
  &\approx \frac{34.0}{2.67} \\
t &\approx 12.7 \\
\\
P &= \mathbb{P}(T_{58} > 12.7) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{P-value to check if 12.7 is reasonable}} \\
P &\approx 0 \implies \space\text{reject H0} \space_\blacksquare &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{strong evidence that $X_1$'s RMR}} \\
&\space &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{is significantly higher than $X_2$'s}} \\

\end{align*}
$$

## 10.33

:::{.question}

> A study was conducted to see if increasing the substrate
> concentration has an appreciable effect on the velocity of a
> chemical reaction. With a substrate concentration of 1.5 moles per
> liter, the reaction was run 15 times, with an average velocity of
> 7.5 micromoles per 30 minutes and a standard deviation of 1.5. With
> a substrate concentration of 2.0 moles per liter, 12 runs were
> made, yielding an average velocity of 8.8 micromoles per 30 minutes
> and a sample standard deviation of 1.2. Is there any reason to
> believe that this increase in substrate concentration causes an
> increase in the mean velocity of the reaction of more than 0.5
> micromole per 30 minutes? Use a 0.01 level of significance and
> assume the populations to be approximately normally distributed
> with equal variances.

:::

<details><summary>hint</summary>

Same tool as 10.27 — `notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.5 pooled t-test — but note this one has a nonzero $d_0$ (testing an increase of _more than_ 0.5, not just any difference) & a fixed significance level rather than a P-value; see &sect;10.2's one-/two-tailed theorem for setting up the critical region.

</details>
<details><summary>plan</summary>

1. Identify the hypotheses & note this tests a _specific effect size_, not just any difference: `H0: μ2 - μ1 = 0.5`, `H1: μ2 - μ1 > 0.5` (does the concentration increase raise velocity by _more than_ 0.5) — one-tailed (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1 def 10.1, &sect;10.2).

2. Normal populations, equal variances assumed, both `σ`'s unknown, small samples — pooled t-test (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.5), same justification as 10.27 (`notes/read-ch_08.md` &sect;8.6).

3. Compute the pooled variance: $s_p^2 = \frac{14(1.5^2)+11(1.2^2)}{25} \approx 1.894$, $s_p \approx 1.376$.

4. Compute the test statistic, subtracting the nonzero $d_0 = 0.5$ (not 0) from the observed difference: $t = \frac{(8.8-7.5)-0.5}{s_p\sqrt{1/15+1/12}} \approx \frac{0.8}{0.533} \approx 1.50$, $v = 15+12-2=25$.

5. Since a fixed significance level `α = 0.01` is given (not a P-value), compare against the critical value from a `t`-table: $t_{0.01}(25) \approx 2.485$ (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.2's hypothesis-test/CI-equivalence framing is the conceptual link between critical-value and P-value approaches).

6. Since $t \approx 1.50 < 2.485$, fail to reject `H0` — insufficient evidence that the increase exceeds 0.5 micromole at `α = 0.01`.

</details>

6. Since $t \approx 2.55 > 1.325$, reject `H0` — evidence supports that VW's fuel economy exceeds Toyota's by more than 4 km/L.

</details>

## 10.45

> radial tires instead of regular belted tires improves fuel economy.
> Twelve cars were equipped with radial tires and driven over a
> prescribed test course. Without changing drivers, the same cars
> were then equipped with regular belted tires and driven once again
> over the test course. The gasoline consumption, in kilometers per
> liter, was recorded as follows:
>
> | Car | Radial Tires | Belted Tires |
> | --- | ------------ | ------------ |
> | 1   | 4.2          | 4.1          |
> | 2   | 4.7          | 4.9          |
> | 3   | 6.6          | 6.2          |
> | 4   | 7.0          | 6.9          |
> | 5   | 6.7          | 6.8          |
> | 6   | 4.5          | 4.4          |
> | 7   | 5.7          | 5.7          |
> | 8   | 6.0          | 5.8          |
> | 9   | 7.4          | 6.9          |
> | 10  | 4.9          | 4.7          |
> | 11  | 6.1          | 6.0          |
> | 12  | 5.2          | 4.9          |
>
> Can we conclude that cars equipped with radial tires give better
> fuel economy than those equipped with belted tires? Assume the
> populations to be normally distributed. Use a P-value in your
> conclusion.

:::

<details><summary>hint</summary>

This is paired data (same cars, two tire types) — `notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.5, paired t-test, not the two-independent-sample version. Compare against `notes/read-ch_09.md` &sect;9.9 (CI for paired observations) for why pairing changes the degrees of freedom.

</details>
<details><summary>plan</summary>

1. Recognize the data structure first: the _same_ 12 cars, same drivers, same course — only the tire type changes. That's paired data, so reduce to a one-sample problem on the differences $d_i$ (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.5, paired t-test theorem) rather than treating the two tire-type columns as independent samples.

2. Compute the 12 differences $d_i = \text{radial}_i - \text{belted}_i$, then their mean $\bar d$ and standard deviation $s_d$ (ch1 &sect;1.3/1.4, applied to the differences rather than the raw data).

3. Set up the hypotheses on the _mean difference_: `H0: μ_D = 0`, `H1: μ_D > 0` (radial gives higher km/liter than belted) — one-tailed (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1, &sect;10.2).

4. Compute the test statistic: $t = \bar d / (s_d/\sqrt n)$, $v = n-1 = 11$ — the `t`-distribution applies here via the same reasoning as corollary 8.1 in `notes/read-ch_08.md` &sect;8.6, just applied to the differences instead of a raw sample.

5. Find the P-value (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1 def 10.5): $P = \mathbb P(T_{11} > t_{\text{obs}})$ — small (roughly in the 0.01-0.025 range).

6. Conclude: small P-value → reject `H0` — evidence supports that radial tires give better fuel economy than belted tires.

</details>

## 10.57

:::{.question}

> A new radar device is being considered for a certain missile
> defense system. The system is checked by experimenting with
> aircraft in which a kill or a no kill is simulated. If, in 300
> trials, 250 kills occur, accept or reject, at the 0.04 level of
> significance, the claim that the probability of a kill with the new
> system does not exceed the 0.8 probability of the existing device.

:::

<details><summary>hint</summary>

`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.8, z-test for a single proportion. &sect;10.2's one-/two-tailed theorem matters here since the claim is one-directional ("does not exceed").

</details>
<details><summary>plan</summary>

1. Identify the hypotheses. The claim under test is that the new system's kill-probability does _not_ exceed 0.8 — frame `H0: p = 0.8` against `H1: p > 0.8` (does the data show it's actually higher, contradicting the claim?) — one-tailed (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1 def 10.1, &sect;10.2).

2. `n = 300` is large and $p_0 = 0.8$ isn't near 0 or 1, so the normal approximation to the binomial applies (`notes/read-ch_06.md` theorem 6.3) — use the z-test for a single proportion (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.8). The underlying count of kills is a binomial random variable (`notes/read-ch_05.md` &sect;5.2).

3. Compute $\hat p = x/n = 250/300 \approx 0.833$, then $z = \frac{x - np_0}{\sqrt{np_0q_0}} = \frac{250-240}{\sqrt{300(0.8)(0.2)}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{48}} \approx 1.44$.

4. Since `α = 0.04` is given (fixed significance level, not P-value), find the critical value from the standard normal table (`notes/read-ch_06.md` &sect;6.3): $z_{0.04} \approx 1.75$.

5. Since $z \approx 1.44 < 1.75$, fail to reject `H0` — insufficient evidence the new system's kill probability exceeds 0.8; the claim stands unrejected.

</details>

## 10.61

:::{.question}

> In a winter of an epidemic flu, the parents of 2000 babies were
> surveyed by researchers at a well-known pharmaceutical company to
> determine if the company's new medicine was effective after two
> days. Among 120 babies who had the flu and were given the medicine,
> 29 were cured within two days. Among 280 babies who had the flu but
> were not given the medicine, 56 recovered within two days. Is there
> any significant indication that supports the company's claim of the
> effectiveness of the medicine?

:::

<details><summary>hint</summary>

`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.9, z-test for two proportions (pooled $\hat p$ ).

</details>
<details><summary>plan</summary>

1. Identify the hypotheses. The company's claim is that the medicine is _effective_ (raises the cure rate), so `H0: p1 = p2`, `H1: p1 > p2` (medicated group cures more often) — one-tailed (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1, &sect;10.2).

2. Two independent samples, both large — z-test for two proportions (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.9). Because `H0` asserts a _common_ `p`, pool the two sample proportions into one estimate rather than using $\hat p_1, \hat p_2$ separately.

3. Compute $\hat p_1 = 29/120 \approx 0.242$, $\hat p_2 = 56/280 = 0.20$, pooled $\hat p = (29+56)/(120+280) = 85/400 \approx 0.213$.

4. Compute the test statistic: $z = \frac{\hat p_1 - \hat p_2}{\sqrt{\hat p\hat q(1/n_1+1/n_2)}} \approx \frac{0.0417}{0.0446} \approx 0.93$.

5. Find the P-value (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1 def 10.5): $P = \mathbb P(Z > 0.93) \approx 1 - 0.8238 = 0.176$ (`notes/read-ch_06.md` &sect;6.3 table lookup).

6. Conclude: a P-value of 0.176 is not small by any conventional standard — fail to reject `H0`; the data don't provide significant evidence the medicine is effective.

</details>

## 10.69

> Aflotoxins produced by mold on peanut crops in Virginia must be
> monitored. A sample of 64 batches of peanuts reveals levels of 24.17
> ppm, on average, with a variance of 4.25 ppm. Test the hypothesis
> that `σ² = 4.2` ppm against the alternative that `σ² ≠ 4.2` ppm. Use

:::

<details><summary>hint</summary>

</details>

1. Identify the hypotheses on the _variance_, not the mean: `H0: σ² = 4.2`, `H1: σ² ≠ 4.2` — two-tailed (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1, &sect;10.2), since the alternative just says "not equal."

3. Compute the test statistic: $\chi^2 = \frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2} = \frac{63(4.25)}{4.2} \approx 63.75$, with $v = n-1 = 63$ degrees of freedom.

5. Find the (two-tailed) P-value (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1 def 10.5) from a chi-squared table/software — it comes out very large (well above any conventional `α`).

6. Conclude: large P-value → fail to reject `H0` — the data are entirely consistent with the claimed $\sigma^2 = 4.2$.

> A machine is supposed to mix peanuts, hazelnuts, cashews, and pecans

5. Since `α = 0.05` is given, compare against the critical value $\chi^2_{0.05}(3) \approx 7.815$.

</details>

## 10.93

:::{.question}

> To determine current attitudes about prayer in public schools, a
> survey was conducted in four Virginia counties. The following table
> gives the attitudes of 200 parents from Craig County, 150 parents
> from Giles County, 100 parents from Franklin County, and 100
> parents from Montgomery County:
>
> | Attitude   | Craig | Giles | Franklin | Mont. |
> | ---------- | ----- | ----- | -------- | ----- |
> | Favor      | 65    | 66    | 40       | 34    |
> | Oppose     | 42    | 30    | 33       | 42    |
> | No opinion | 93    | 54    | 27       | 24    |
>
> Test for homogeneity of attitudes among the four counties concerning
> prayer in the public schools. Use a P-value in your conclusion.

:::

<details><summary>hint</summary>

`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.13, chi-squared test for homogeneity (fixed sample sizes drawn from four distinct county populations — contrast with the independence test in &sect;10.12, which is mechanically identical but assumes a different sampling design).

</details>
<details><summary>plan</summary>

1. Recognize the sampling design first, since it decides which chi-squared test applies: each county's sample size (200, 150, 100, 100) was fixed _before_ sampling, not left to chance — that's the signal for the homogeneity test (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.13), not the independence test (&sect;10.12), even though the arithmetic is identical.

2. Build the 3×4 contingency table (attitude × county) and compute row totals, column totals, and the grand total `N`.

3. Compute expected counts for every cell: $e_{ij} = (\text{row total}_i)(\text{column total}_j)/N$ (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.13).

4. Compute $\chi^2 = \sum_{i,j}(o_{ij}-e_{ij})^2/e_{ij}$ over all 12 cells.

5. Degrees of freedom: $v = (r-1)(c-1) = (3-1)(4-1) = 6$.

6. Find the P-value (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1 def 10.5) from a chi-squared table with $v=6$ — it comes out very small (well under 0.001), since $\chi^2_{\text{obs}}$ ends up far out in the tail.

7. Conclude: reject `H0` — attitudes toward prayer in public schools are not homogeneous across the four counties.

## 10.101

:::{.question}

> In a study analyzed by the Statistics Consulting Center at Virginia
> Tech, a group of subjects was asked to complete a certain task on
> the computer. The response measured was the time to completion. The
> purpose of the experiment was to test a set of facilitation tools
> developed by the Department of Computer Science at the university.
> There were 10 subjects involved. With a random assignment, five
> were given a standard procedure using Fortran language for
> completion of the task. The other five were asked to do the task
> with the use of the facilitation tools. The data on the completion
> times for the task are given here.
>
> | Group 1 (Standard Procedure) | Group 2 (Facilitation Tool) |
> | ---------------------------- | --------------------------- |
> | 161                          | 132                         |
> | 169                          | 162                         |
> | 174                          | 134                         |
> | 158                          | 138                         |
> | 163                          | 133                         |
>
> Assuming that the population distributions are normal and variances
> are the same for the two groups, support or refute the conjecture
> that the facilitation tools increase the speed with which the task
> can be accomplished.

:::

<details><summary>hint</summary>

Despite the two-column table layout, this is _not_ paired data — five subjects were randomly assigned to each group independently. Use `notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.5, pooled t-test for two means (equal variances stated), not the paired t-test (contrast with 10.45).

</details>
<details><summary>plan</summary>

1. Confirm the sample structure first (this is the crux of the problem): 10 subjects were _randomly assigned_, five to each group — there's no shared unit linking a Group-1 row to a Group-2 row, so this is two **independent** samples despite the side-by-side table, unlike 10.45's genuinely paired design.

2. Identify the hypotheses: the conjecture is the facilitation tool _speeds up_ the task, i.e. lowers completion time, so with Group 1 = standard, Group 2 = tool: `H0: μ1 = μ2`, `H1: μ1 > μ2` — one-tailed (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1, &sect;10.2).

3. Normal populations, equal variances stated, small `n1 = n2 = 5` — pooled t-test (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.5), same tool as 10.27/10.33/10.37.

4. Compute $\bar X_1, \bar X_2, s_1^2, s_2^2$ (ch1 &sect;1.3/1.4) for each group, then the pooled variance $s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}$.

5. Compute the test statistic $t = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1+1/n_2}}$, with $v = n_1+n_2-2 = 8$ degrees of freedom.

6. Find the P-value (`notes/read-ch_10.md` &sect;10.1 def 10.5) — comes out quite small (comfortably under 0.01).

7. Conclude: reject `H0` — the data support the conjecture that the facilitation tools increase task-completion speed.
