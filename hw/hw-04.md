:::{.question}

> The probability distribution of $X$, the number of imperfections per
> 10 meters of a synthetic fabric in continuous rolls of uniform width,
> is given in Exercise 3.13 as
>
> | $x$    | 0    | 1    | 2    | 3    | 4    |
> | ------ | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- | ---- |
> | $f(x)$ | 0.41 | 0.37 | 0.16 | 0.05 | 0.01 |
>

$$
\begin{align*}

\mu &= E(X) = \sum_x x f(x) &&

    &= (0.41)0 + (0.37)1 + (0.16)2 + (0.05)3 + (0.01)4 \\
\mu &= 0.88 \space_\blacksquare

\end{align*}
$$

## 2

:::{.question}

> The probability distribution of the discrete random variable $X$ is
>
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> f(x) = \binom{3}{x} \left(\frac14\right)^x \left(\frac34\right)^{3-x}, \quad x = 0, 1, 2, 3.
> \end{align*}
> $$
>
> Find the mean of $X$.

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

f(0) &= \binom{3}{0} \left(\tfrac14\right)^0 \left(\tfrac34\right)^3 = \tfrac{27}{64} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.1\}}} \\
f(1) &= \binom{3}{1} \left(\tfrac14\right)^1 \left(\tfrac34\right)^2 = \tfrac{27}{64} \\
f(2) &= \binom{3}{2} \left(\tfrac14\right)^2 \left(\tfrac34\right)^1 = \tfrac{9}{64} \\
f(3) &= \binom{3}{3} \left(\tfrac14\right)^3 \left(\tfrac34\right)^0 = \tfrac{1}{64} \\
\\

\mu &= E(X) = \sum_x x f(x) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.1\}}} \\
    &= (0)\tfrac{27}{64} + (1)\tfrac{27}{64} + (2)\tfrac{9}{64} + (3)\tfrac{1}{64} \\
    &= \frac{0 + 27 + 18 + 3}{64} = \frac{48}{64} \\
\mu &= \frac34 \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$

## 11

:::{.question}

> The density function of coded measurements of the pitch diameter of
> threads of a fitting is
>
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> f(x) = \begin{cases}
>          \frac{4}{\pi (1+x^2)}, &0 < x < 1, \\
>          0,                     &\text{elsewhere}.
>        \end{cases} \tag{11.1}
> \end{align*}
> $$
>
> Find the expected value of $X$.

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

\mu &= E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x) dx &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.1\}}} \\
\mu &= \int_0^1 x \frac{4}{\pi (1+x^2)} dx = \frac{4}{\pi} \int_0^1 \frac{x}{1+x^2} dx &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{11.1\}, $x \leq 0$ or $x \geq 1$ both $= 0$}} \\
    &= \frac{4}{\pi} \int_0^1 \frac{1}{u} \frac{1}{2} du = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^1 \frac{1}{u} du &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{where $u = 1 + x^2$ \& $du = 2x dx \implies \frac{1}{2} du = x dx$}} \\
    &= \frac{2}{\pi} [ln u]_0^1 = \frac{2}{\pi} [ln (1 + x^2)]_0^1 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{subsitute back in for $u$}} \\
    &= \frac{2}{\pi} [ln (1 + 1^2) - ln (1 + 0^2)] = \frac{2}{\pi} [ln 2 - ln 1] = \frac{2}{\pi} ln 2 \\
\mu &\approx 0.4413 \space_\blacksquare

\end{align*}
$$

## 16

:::{.question}

> Suppose that you are inspecting a lot of 1000 light bulbs, among
> which 20 are defectives. You choose two light bulbs randomly from
> the lot without replacement. Let
>
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> X_1 = \begin{cases}
>         1, &\text{if the 1st light bulb is defective}, \\
>         0, &\text{otherwise},
>       \end{cases} \qquad
> X_2 = \begin{cases}
>         1, &\text{if the 2nd light bulb is defective}, \\
>         0, &\text{otherwise}.
>       \end{cases}
> \end{align*}
> $$
>
> Find the probability that at least one light bulb chosen is
> defective. [Hint: Compute $P(X_1 + X_2 = 1)$.]

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

\mathbb{P}(\text{at least 1 defective}) &= \mathbb{P}(X_1 + X_2 = 1) + \mathbb{P}(X_1 + X_2 = 2) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{disjoint events \{Walpole corollary 2.1\}}} \\
\\

\mathbb{P}(X_1 + X_2 = 1) &= \mathbb{P}(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 0) + \mathbb{P}(X_1 = 0, X_2 = 1) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{2 disjoint ways to draw exactly 1 defective}} \\
  &= \left(\tfrac{20}{1000}\right)\left(\tfrac{980}{999}\right)
   + \left(\tfrac{980}{1000}\right)\left(\tfrac{20}{999}\right) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole theorem 2.10\}, sampling without replacement}} \\
  &= \frac{2(20)(980)}{(1000)(999)} = \frac{39200}{999000} \\
\mathbb{P}(X_1 + X_2 = 1) &\approx 0.03924 \tag{16.1} \\
\\

\mathbb{P}(X_1 + X_2 = 2) &= \mathbb{P}(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 1) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{both bulbs defective}} \\
  &= \left(\tfrac{20}{1000}\right)\left(\tfrac{19}{999}\right) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole theorem 2.10\}}} \\
  &= \frac{380}{999000} \\
\mathbb{P}(X_1 + X_2 = 2) &\approx 0.00038 \tag{16.2} \\
\\

\mathbb{P}(\text{at least 1 defective}) &\approx 0.03924 + 0.00038 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{16.1, 16.2\}}} \\
\mathbb{P}(\text{at least 1 defective}) &\approx 0.0396 \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$

\end{align*}

## 30

:::{.question}

> In Exercise 3.31, the distribution of times before a major repair of
> a washing machine was given as
>
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> f(y) = \begin{cases}
>          \frac14 e^{-y/4}, &y \geq 0, \\
>          0,                &\text{elsewhere}.
>        \end{cases} \tag{30.1}
> \end{align*}
> $$
>
> What is the population mean of the times to repair?

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

u &= y, \quad dv = \tfrac14 e^{-y/4} dy \implies du = dy, \quad v = -e^{-y/4} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{integration by parts, used below}} \\
\int y \left(\tfrac14 e^{-y/4}\right) dy &= -y e^{-y/4} + \int e^{-y/4} dy \\
                                        &= -y e^{-y/4} - 4 e^{-y/4} \\
                                        &= -(y + 4) e^{-y/4} \tag{30.2} \\
\\

\mu &= E(Y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y f(y) dy &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.1\}}} \\
    &= \int_0^{\infty} y \left(\tfrac14 e^{-y/4}\right) dy &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{30.1\}, $f(y) = 0$ for $y < 0$}} \\
    &= [-(y+4)e^{-y/4}]_0^{\infty} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{30.2\}}} \\
    &= 0 - (-4) \\
\mu &= 4 \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$

## 34

:::{.question}

> Let $X$ be a random variable with the following probability
> distribution:
>
> | $x$    | -2  | 3   | 5   |
> | ------ | --- | --- | --- |
> | $f(x)$ | 0.3 | 0.2 | 0.5 |
>
> Find the standard deviation of $X$.

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

\mu &= E(X) = \sum_x x f(x) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.1\}}} \\
    &= (-2)(0.3) + (3)(0.2) + (5)(0.5) \\
    &= -0.6 + 0.6 + 2.5 \\
\mu &= 2.5 \tag{34.1} \\
\\

E(X^2) &= \sum_x x^2 f(x) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole theorem 4.1, $g(x) = x^2$\}}} \\
       &= (-2)^2(0.3) + (3)^2(0.2) + (5)^2(0.5) \\
       &= 1.2 + 1.8 + 12.5 \\
E(X^2) &= 15.5 \tag{34.2} \\
\\

\sigma^2 &= E(X^2) - \mu^2 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole theorem 4.2\}}} \\
         &= 15.5 - (2.5)^2 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{34.1, 34.2\}}} \\
\sigma^2 &= 9.25 \\
\\

\sigma &= \sqrt{\sigma^2} = \sqrt{9.25} \\
\sigma &\approx 3.041 \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$

## 48

:::{.question}

> Given a random variable $X$, with standard deviation $\sigma_X$, and
> a random variable $Y = a + bX$, show that if $b < 0$, the correlation
> coefficient $\rho_{XY} = -1$, and if $b > 0$, $\rho_{XY} = 1$.

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

\mu_Y &= E(a + bX) = a + b\mu_X &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole theorem 4.5\}}} \\
Y - \mu_Y &= (a + bX) - (a + b\mu_X) = b(X - \mu_X) \tag{48.1} \\
\\

\sigma_{XY} &= E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)] &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.4\}}} \\
            &= E[(X - \mu_X) \cdot b(X - \mu_X)] &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{48.1\}}} \\
            &= b \, E[(X - \mu_X)^2] \\
\sigma_{XY} &= b \sigma_X^2 \tag{48.2} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.3\}}} \\
\\

\sigma_Y^2 &= \sigma^2_{a+bX} = b^2 \sigma_X^2 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole corollary 4.6\}}} \\
\sigma_Y &= \sqrt{b^2 \sigma_X^2} = |b|\, \sigma_X \tag{48.3} \\
\\

\rho_{XY} &= \frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.5\}}} \\
          &= \frac{b \sigma_X^2}{\sigma_X \cdot |b| \sigma_X} &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{48.2, 48.3\}}} \\
\rho_{XY} &= \frac{b}{|b|} \tag{48.4} \\
\\

\rho_{XY} &= \frac{b}{|b|} = 1, \quad \text{if } b > 0 &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{48.4\}}} \\
\rho_{XY} &= \frac{b}{|b|} = -1, \quad \text{if } b < 0 \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$

## 60

:::{.question}

> Suppose that $X$ and $Y$ are independent random variables having the
> joint probability distribution
>
> | $f(x,y)$ | $x=2$ | $x=4$ |
> | -------- | ----- | ----- |
> | $y=1$    | 0.10  | 0.15  |
> | $y=3$    | 0.20  | 0.30  |
> | $y=5$    | 0.10  | 0.15  |
>
> Find \
> (a) $E(2X - 3Y)$; \
> (b) $E(XY)$.

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

g(x) &= \sum_y f(x,y) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 3.10\}, column totals}} \\
g(2) &= 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.10 = 0.40 \\
g(4) &= 0.15 + 0.30 + 0.15 = 0.60 \\
\\

h(y) &= \sum_x f(x,y) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 3.10\}, row totals}} \\
h(1) &= 0.10 + 0.15 = 0.25 \\
h(3) &= 0.20 + 0.30 = 0.50 \\
h(5) &= 0.10 + 0.15 = 0.25 \\
\\

\mu_X &= \sum_x x g(x) = (2)(0.40) + (4)(0.60) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.1\}}} \\
\mu_X &= 3.2 \tag{60.1} \\
\mu_Y &= \sum_y y h(y) = (1)(0.25) + (3)(0.50) + (5)(0.25) \\
\mu_Y &= 3.0 \tag{60.2} \\
\\

E(2X - 3Y) &= 2E(X) - 3E(Y) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{(a) \{Walpole corollary 4.4, extended to a linear combination\}}} \\
           &= 2\mu_X - 3\mu_Y \\
           &= 2(3.2) - 3(3.0) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{60.1, 60.2\}}} \\
           &= 6.4 - 9.0 \\
E(2X - 3Y) &= -2.6 \space_\blacksquare \\
\\

E(XY) &= E(X)E(Y) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{(b) $X, Y$ independent \{Walpole theorem 4.8\}}} \\
      &= \mu_X \mu_Y = (3.2)(3.0) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{60.1, 60.2\}}} \\
E(XY) &= 9.6 \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$

:::{.question}

> Suppose that $X$ and $Y$ are independent random variables with
> probability densities
>
> $$
> \begin{align*}
> g(x) = \begin{cases}
>          \frac{8}{x^3}, &x > 2, \\

>        \end{cases}
> \end{align*} \tag{64.1}
> $$
>

:::

$$
\begin{align*}

E(X) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x g(x) dx = \int_2^{\infty} x \left(\tfrac{8}{x^3}\right) dx &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.1\}, \{64.1\}}} \\
     &= \int_2^{\infty} \frac{8}{x^2} dx = [-\tfrac{8}{x}]_2^{\infty} \\
     &= 0 - (-4) \\
E(X) &= 4 \tag{64.2} \\
\\

E(Y) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y h(y) dy = \int_0^1 y (2y) dy &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{Walpole def. 4.1\}, \{64.1\}}} \\
     &= \int_0^1 2y^2 dy = [\tfrac23 y^3]_0^1 \\
E(Y) &= \tfrac23 \tag{64.3} \\
\\

E(Z) &= E(XY) = E(X)E(Y) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{$X, Y$ independent \{Walpole theorem 4.8\}}} \\
     &= (4)\left(\tfrac23\right) &&
  \htmlClass{hljs-comment}{\textit{\{64.2, 64.3\}}} \\
E(Z) &= \tfrac83 \approx 2.667 \space_\blacksquare \\

\end{align*}
$$
